5 Costly Mistakes Expats Make When Sending Money to/from Japan

Updated April 14, 2026

Kyoto Station at 8 p.m. The last train left ten minutes ago. A salaryman in a rumpled suit stares at the departure board while clutching a receipt from a bank counter inside the station.

His mistake wasn’t leaving it to the last minute. It was the receipt itself.

That 2,000 yen fee to send 500,000 yen to Bangkok? It could have been 800 yen. He paid for the convenience of the station branch, the same convenience that turns a 10-minute walk into a 45-minute ordeal after midnight.

The train finally arrives. He gets on. The carriage smells faintly of cha-ahan and sweat. He checks his phone. Another notification: the transfer he tried to cancel is now pending.

The next morning, he opens his laptop. Wise shows the same transaction at half the cost. He realizes he’s been paying double what he should for years.

This scene happens every week in Tokyo and Osaka. People arrive in Japan with fresh visas, open bank accounts, and immediately learn that their shiny new passbook savings account is not designed for international transfers.

The first foreign card I ever used was a UnionPay debit tied to my Chinese bank. I thought it was clever. Then I tried to send money to my US account using the ATM in Shinjuku. The machine laughed. Not literally. But the 3,000 yen fee and 48-hour hold told me everything I needed to know.

That day, I learned three things at once. Japanese bank ATMs are not friends to foreigners sending money abroad. UnionPay works, but the fees are punitive. And the shiny app on my phone—Revolut—had a better deal waiting in its settings menu.

I still keep a Japanese savings account. It’s where my salary lands, where I pay rent, where I buy groceries with that peculiar IC card that beeps when I tap too slowly. But for anything that leaves Japan, I use something else. Wise for speed. Revolut for weekends. Sony Bank for yen when the exchange rate looks delicious.

The mistake most expats make is thinking their Japanese bank will handle everything. They walk into the branch with a smile and a passport, expecting the same low fees they saw on TV. They leave with a 3% fee and a lecture about KYC.

Let me tell you what they don’t tell you in the welcome seminar at the immigration office.

Mistake 1: Assuming your Japanese bank’s international transfer is cheap

Mitsubishi UFJ, Mizuho, SMBC—the big three Japanese banks love to advertise “international remittances with ease.” The problem is the fine print. The ease costs money.

As of early 2026, sending 100,000 yen from MUFG to a US bank typically costs around 4,000 yen in fees plus the mid-market rate spread. That spread—the difference between the rate you see on Google and the rate the bank gives you—can be 2 to 3%. On 100,000 yen, that’s another 2,000 to 3,000 yen hidden in the exchange.

Wise, by comparison, charges about 700 yen in fees plus the mid-market rate. The total cost drops from roughly 6,000 yen to 700 yen. That’s not a small difference. That’s the difference between eating out once a week and skipping meals.

I learned this the hard way when I tried to send money to my German account for a deposit on an apartment. My bank’s online form insisted I fill out a “purpose of remittance” field. I wrote “rent deposit” in six kanji. The clerk at the branch still called me two days later to confirm. Meanwhile, Wise processed the same transfer in under an hour with no phone calls.

The Japanese banks don’t hide their fees. They bury them in the rate. The mid-market rate is what you see on XE.com. Your bank gives you something worse. The difference? That’s their fee.

And if you walk into a branch? The fee multiplies. Some branches add a “counseling fee” that feels like a tip for breathing the same air as the clerk.

Mistake 2: Using your home country’s bank card in Japan for transfers

I once watched a colleague from the UK try to send money from his Halifax debit card to his girlfriend in Australia. He used the ATM at Shibuya station, tapped “international transfer,” and waited. The machine spat out a receipt with a fee of £35 plus a 3% currency conversion charge.

He didn’t know that Halifax charges 3% for non-sterling transactions. He also didn’t know that Japanese ATMs are not designed for foreign cards sending money abroad. They want you to use their network, their rates, their timing.

Revolut solved this for me. I loaded my Japanese salary onto a Revolut card. When I need to send money home, I tap “Send” inside the app, choose the destination, and pay the real exchange rate. No ATM machines. No branch visits. No 3% surcharge.

But Revolut has limits. For large transfers—say, 5 million yen or more—Wise becomes cheaper. Revolut’s free tier has a 1,000 euro monthly transfer limit. Once you hit that, the fee jumps to 0.5% plus 0.5 euros. On 100,000 yen, that’s about 500 yen. Wise charges 700 yen flat. So for small amounts, Revolut wins. For large amounts, Wise wins.

The mistake is assuming your home bank’s card will work the same way in Japan as it does at home. It won’t. The ATM network, the currency conversion, the daily limits—all optimized for Japanese users, not foreigners sending money out of Japan.

Pro tip: If you’re expecting a large transfer soon—say, from selling a car back home—set up both Wise and Revolut in advance. Test small transfers first. Check the rates on Google at the exact moment you plan to send. The difference between 145.5 yen per dollar and 145.8 yen per dollar can mean thousands of yen on a million-yen transfer.

I did this before wiring my US stimulus check into yen. I watched the rate for three days. On the fourth day, I hit send at 146.2 yen. The same transfer two days earlier would have cost me 6,000 yen more.

The trick isn’t timing the market perfectly. The trick is knowing your tools and having them ready before the rate moves in the wrong direction.

Mistake 3: Ignoring the tax paperwork for large transfers

Last year, a friend in Fukuoka tried to send 8 million yen to his parents in the Philippines. He used Wise. The transfer went through. The money arrived. Then his mother called: the bank in Manila froze the funds and asked for proof of income.

Turns out, transfers over 1 million yen from Japan to certain countries trigger an automatic tax inquiry. The Japanese tax office flags the transfer. The receiving bank, especially in the Philippines, Brazil, or China, may ask for proof that the money is not income or gift tax evasion.

The tax office isn’t trying to ruin your life. They’re following international agreements to prevent money laundering. But the paperwork can take weeks. Sometimes months. Your parents may need to show tax returns from Japan or a letter from your employer.

I learned this when I tried to send 3 million yen to my sister in Thailand for her wedding. I filled out Wise’s form, clicked send, and received an email two days later: “Your transfer has been flagged for tax review. Please upload supporting documents.”

I uploaded my residence card, my tax certificate, and a letter from my employer. It took 10 days. During that time, my sister’s wedding planner kept calling asking when the down payment would arrive. I had to promise to cover the venue fee from my own savings until the transfer cleared.

The mistake is assuming large transfers are as simple as typing a number and hitting send. They’re not. The tax office watches. The receiving bank watches. And if the amount looks like income or a gift, they ask questions.

Pro tip: If you plan to send more than 1 million yen in a single transfer—or more than 10 million yen in a year—check the tax rules of both countries first. In the Philippines, for example, gifts over 100,000 pesos (about 300,000 yen) require gift tax returns. In Brazil, transfers over 10,000 reais may trigger a CPF update and tax form.

Keep copies of your Japanese tax filings. Some countries ask for proof that the money was already taxed in Japan. Without it, they may tax it again.

Also, Wise and Revolut both have tax forms you can download in advance. Fill them out before you send. It saves days.

Mistake 4: Not checking the receiving bank’s cut

I once sent 500,000 yen to a friend in Vietnam using Sony Bank’s international wire. The transfer took two days. The money arrived. He texted: “Where is the rest?”

I checked the receipt. Sony Bank charged 2,500 yen. The receiving bank in Hanoi—BIDV—charged another 50,000 dong (about 300 yen). But the real thief was the correspondent bank in Singapore. They took 15 USD (about 2,200 yen) as an intermediary fee.

Total deductions: nearly 5,000 yen. On a 500,000 yen transfer, that’s 1%. Small, but annoying. Multiply it by a dozen transfers a year, and it becomes a round-trip ticket to nowhere.

Revolut and Wise both promise “no hidden fees.” But they can’t control the receiving bank’s appetite. If the recipient’s bank is in Vietnam, Nigeria, or Brazil, expect a fee. If it’s in the US, UK, or EU, the fee may be zero.

The mistake is assuming the transfer is complete once it leaves your hands. It’s not. The money is in transit. And every stop along the way—correspondent banks, receiving banks, currency converters—takes a slice.

Pro tip: Ask the recipient for their bank’s SWIFT code and the exact account details. Then check the receiving bank’s website for their fee schedule. Some banks in the Philippines charge 500 pesos flat. Others charge 1% of the amount. The difference matters.

I do this now. Before I send, I ask: “What’s your bank’s fee for international wires?” If it’s high, I adjust the amount I send. If the recipient needs 500,000 yen, I send 505,000 yen so they get what they asked for.

Mistake 5: Forgetting weekend and holiday timing

The worst transfer I ever made was on a Saturday night. I was in a izakaya in Golden Gai, celebrating a new job. I got an email: “Your transfer is pending bank approval.” I didn’t think much of it. I tapped “Send” and went back to my beer.

On Monday morning, I checked. The transfer was still pending. The bank’s system was closed. Not just the branch. The entire backend system for international wires shuts down on weekends and Japanese holidays.

Wise and Revolut don’t have this problem. Their systems run 24/7. But if you use a Japanese bank, your transfer may sit in a queue until Tuesday morning. If the transfer is urgent—say, rent deposit or emergency family needs—you’re out of luck.

The mistake is assuming all services work the same on weekends and holidays. They don’t. Japanese banks treat weekends like they treated women’s suffrage in 1945: reluctantly, and only under pressure.

Pro tip: Plan transfers for weekdays during business hours. If you must send on a weekend, use Wise or Revolut. They process transfers even when the Nikkei is closed and the salaryman is asleep.

The morning after the izakaya disaster, I woke up to a Wise notification: “Transfer completed. 475,000 yen credited to recipient.” I checked the time. 3:17 a.m. on Sunday. The money moved while I was snoring through the last train home.

I still use my Japanese bank. It’s where my salary lands, where I pay rent, where I buy groceries with that peculiar IC card that beeps when I tap too slowly. But for anything that leaves Japan, I use something else. Wise for speed. Revolut for weekends. Sony Bank for yen when the exchange rate looks delicious.

The mistake isn’t using the wrong tool. The mistake is using only one tool. Japan’s banking system works beautifully—for Japanese people sending money to other Japanese people. For the rest of us, the system is a maze of fees, paperwork, and polite but unhelpful clerks.

The solution isn’t to stop using Japanese banks. It’s to use them wisely. Keep an account for daily life. But for anything that crosses a border—use a specialist. Wise, Revolut, or Sony Bank’s international service. They’re designed for people who don’t speak Japanese, who don’t have time for branch visits, who need the money to arrive before the next rent payment is due.

And if you’re at Kyoto Station at 8 p.m., staring at a receipt with a 2,000 yen fee, remember: the train home isn’t the only thing leaving late tonight. So is your money. And the sooner it leaves, the sooner it arrives.
